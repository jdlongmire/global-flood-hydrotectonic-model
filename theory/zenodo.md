## Title

Global Flood Hydrotectonics: A Quantitative Theory of Rapid Continental Reorganization

---

## Abstract

This preprint presents *Global Flood Hydrotectonics* as a working physical research programme exploring whether rapid, large-scale continental reorganization during a global flood event can be made energetically and thermodynamically coherent under known physical laws. The model addresses the longstanding "heat problem" associated with catastrophic plate motion by proposing a hydraulic rather than mantle-viscous driving mechanism, in which continental blocks undergo hydroplaning along fluid-saturated detachment horizons under conditions of near-lithostatic pore pressure. Using Terzaghi's effective stress principle, order-of-magnitude energy and heat-flux estimates are developed to evaluate whether kilometer-scale motions over year-scale durations are physically admissible without producing lethal global heating. The framework adopts a staged structure that distinguishes stipulated initial conditions from subsequent law-governed dynamics, and it explicitly separates novel, discriminating predictions from post-hoc reinterpretations of existing observations. Quantitative estimates, reproducible calculations, and known uncertainties are presented transparently. The work does not claim confirmation, consensus, or completeness, but aims to establish whether the proposed mechanism constitutes a physically plausible research programme that is exposed to empirical risk and amenable to further testing, refinement, or falsification.

## Description (Extended)

This repository contains a quantitative framework for modeling rapid continental reorganization during a proposed global flood event. The model is built on reproducible Jupyter notebooks and explicitly documents:

- Stipulated initial conditions (Stage 0)
- Law-governed dynamics (Stages 1-2)
- Order-of-magnitude energy and heat budgets
- Known gaps and uncertainties
- Falsification criteria

The work is released for open scientific evaluation and critique. Contributions are welcome, particularly in computational modeling of pressure stability, permeability evolution, thermal feedback, and discriminating predictions.
